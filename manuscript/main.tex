\begin{abstract}
A new non-contact surface-metrology measurement principle is proposed and
analysed.  The measurand is the surface height $z_s(x,y)$ of a conducting
sample; the sensor is a single trapped atomic ion, whose secular-frequency
shift $\Delta\omega_x$ at height $h$ encodes the surface electrostatic
perturbation.  The measurement operator is the instrument transfer
function $H_x(k;h) \propto k_x^2 e^{-kh}$ --- a distinctive
height-tunable band-pass spatial transfer function.  The $e^{-kh}$ factor
imposes an irreversible spatial-information cutoff: quantum enhancement
can maximise the precision of available signals but cannot recover what
the physical filter has destroyed, and the recoverable mode rank scales as
$r_{\rm eff} \propto 1/h$ on the analysed 1D cross-section.  A theorem
establishes the topography--charge identifiability limit: multi-height
scanning alone cannot separate surface topography from surface charge,
which share the identical $e^{-kh}$ scaling, so surface charge is an
influence quantity structurally confounded with the measurand.
The analytical transfer function is independently cross-checked against
exact Rayleigh/Floquet, boundary-element and finite-difference
electrostatic solvers, and evaluated using published atomic-force-microscopy
topography data; the experimental demonstration remains future work.
Measurement sensitivity is distinguished from reconstruction uncertainty:
the latter is charge-limited (combined standard uncertainty $319$\,nm under
the stated residual-charge baseline), whereas the former reaches nanometre
and, at the quantum projection noise limit, picometre scale.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} surface metrology; instrument transfer
function; trapped ion; quantum sensing; inverse problems; measurement
uncertainty

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===================================================================

Surface metrology underpins manufacturing, materials science, and device
fabrication.  The dominant techniques --- atomic force microscopy (AFM),
scanning tunnelling microscopy (STM), optical profilometry, and scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) --- each occupy a well-defined niche in the
resolution--environment parameter space~\cite{iso25178}.  A persistent and
growing need exists, however, for non-contact surface characterisation at
micrometre standoff in ultra-high vacuum (UHV) and at cryogenic
temperatures, driven by the quantum-device community: superconducting
qubit electrode degradation~\cite{oliver2013materials}, ion-trap chip
surface contamination~\cite{brownnutt2015review}, and two-level-system
losses in amorphous dielectrics~\cite{muller2019quantum}.  Although AFM
and STM already operate in situ in UHV and at cryogenic temperatures, they
probe the surface in mechanical contact or tunnelling proximity and can
perturb the very surface fields that matter for qubit performance; a
technique that reads the surface electrostatically from micrometre
standoff, without mechanical contact and natively inside the trap, would
close this gap.

Trapped atomic ions are among the most sensitive detectors of electric
fields realised to date~\cite{keller2015electrometry}.  A single ion
confined in a radio-frequency (rf) Paul trap responds to stray
electrostatic potentials through shifts of its secular motional
frequencies and excess micromotion
amplitude~\cite{leibfried2003review}, and through the motional heating
rate first measured by Turchette et al.~\cite{turchette2000heating}.  The
trapped-ion community has invested two decades in characterising these
surface-induced perturbations as a noise source limiting quantum
coherence, and in eliminating them (reviewed by Brownnutt et
al.~\cite{brownnutt2015review}).  The idea of deliberately using an ion to
probe a surface was pioneered by Maiwald et al.~\cite{maiwald2009stylus},
and recent work by S{\"a}gesser et al.~\cite{sagesser2026scanning} has
extended it to three-dimensional scanning of an ion probe, confirming the
experimental maturity of the platform.

Despite these advances, the quantitative inverse problem --- reconstructing
surface topography $z_s(x,y)$ from ion motional observables with a defined
instrument transfer function, regularised inversion and an uncertainty
budget --- has not, to the present knowledge, been formulated.  This work
addresses that gap.  The proposed technique is named the \textbf{Q}uantum
\textbf{E}lectrostatic \textbf{S}canning \textbf{P}robe
\textbf{M}icroscopy (QESPM).  The contribution is a theoretical framework:
analytically derived, numerically benchmarked against exact
electrostatic solvers, evaluated on experimentally measured surface
topography, and equipped with a GUM-based uncertainty budget and a defined
experimental roadmap; the experimental demonstration itself remains future
work.  The paper is organised around the measurement chain: measurand
definition (Sec.~\ref{sec:concept}); the forward model and instrument
transfer function (Sec.~\ref{sec:forward}); spatial information limits and
the topography--charge identifiability theorem (Sec.~\ref{sec:info});
quantum precision and the metrological uncertainty framework
(Sec.~\ref{sec:precision}); numerical validation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}); the operating envelope, limitations and
implementation roadmap (Sec.~\ref{sec:envelope}); and the metrological
discussion and conclusions (Secs.~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:conclusions}).
Detailed derivations, proofs, implementation details and extended
validation are provided in the Supplementary Material (Secs.~S1--S16).

\section{Measurement concept and metrological measurand}
\label{sec:concept}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Measurand, indication and influence quantities}

The primary measurand of the proposed instrument is the surface height
profile relative to a specified mean reference plane,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\; z_s(x,y) \;=\; \text{surface height relative to the mean reference plane} \;}
\end{equation}
The instrument does not measure $z_s(x,y)$ directly.  It measures the
secular frequency shift $\Delta\omega_x(x,y)$, related to the surface
through the chain
\begin{equation}
z_s \; \longrightarrow \; \Phi \; \longrightarrow \; \Delta\omega_x,
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the electrostatic potential produced by the surface at the
ion position.  In the presence of surface charge $\sigma(x,y)$ the
measurement model is the joint operator
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\; \Delta\omega_x \;=\; \mathcal{H}[\,z_s,\,\sigma\,] \;}
\end{equation}
and surface charge is therefore not simply a noise source: it is an
influence quantity structurally confounded with the measurand
(Sec.~\ref{sec:info}).  The complete quantity hierarchy is summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:quantities}.

\subsection{Measurement geometry and experimental concept}

Consider a single ion of mass $m$ and charge $+e$ confined in a linear
Paul trap with secular frequencies $\omega_x$, $\omega_y$, $\omega_z$,
positioned at height $h$ above a grounded conducting surface whose height
profile is $z_s(x,y)$ relative to the mean plane $z=0$; a uniform
background electric field $E_{\rm bg}\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ arises from the
trap electrodes.  The analytical model treats the local surface region
facing the ion as an infinite grounded conducting half-space at $z=0$:
in the proposed implementation the ion is held stationary in a planar
trap while the sample is translated laterally beneath it, the stage being
arranged so that the local sample region approximates this half-space
(sample extent and scan range $\gg h$, surface plane parallel to the trap
plane).  The lateral translation leaves the local forward model unchanged
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}).

\subsection{Assumptions and validity conditions}

The model rests on assumptions, each with an explicit validity condition
(full analysis and the validity diagram in Supplementary Sec.~S4):
(i)~small surface roughness and slope, $|z_s|\ll h$ and $|\nabla z_s|\ll1$
(for a sinusoid $z_s=A\sin(kx)$: $Ak\ll1$); (ii)~uniform background field
across the field of view; (iii)~perfect conductor (a thin dielectric film
$d$ with permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ modifies the surface-potential
channel by the exact three-layer factor
$F(k)=\varepsilon_r e^{kd}/[\varepsilon_r\cosh(kd)+\sinh(kd)]$, which is
$F-1=+3\times10^{-4}$ for a native oxide at the budget wavenumber and
reaches $+19\%$ for a $2$\,\textmu m polymer overlayer; $F$ is identical
for the topographic and charge channels, so the identifiability theorem
of Sec.~\ref{sec:info} is unaffected);
(iv)~static surface charge over the scan duration (frozen at $T=4$\,K);
(v)~Lamb--Dicke regime ($\Delta x_{\rm ion}\approx13$\,nm $\ll
\lambda_{\rm feature}$);
(vi)~perturbative frequency shift, $|\Delta\omega_x|\ll\omega_x$;
(vii)~negligible Casimir--Polder force ($h\gtrsim100$\,nm; the crossover
at which the C--P signal would equal the electrostatic signal lies at
$h^*\approx30$\,nm for features with $\lambda\approx h$, so the $100$\,nm
exclusion is conservative); and (viii)~micromotion compensated to
$\beta<10^{-4}$ via rf--photon correlation~\cite{berkeland1998}.

\section{QESPM forward model and spatial transfer function}
\label{sec:forward}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Boundary perturbation theory}

For a perfectly conducting surface held at zero potential, the exact
boundary condition is $\Phi(x,y,z_s(x,y))=0$.  Expanding in the small
parameters $|z_s|/h\ll1$ and $|\nabla z_s|\ll1$, the first-order
correction satisfies $\nabla^2\Phi^{(1)}=0$ for $z>0$ with the boundary
perturbation condition~\cite{villarrubia1997tip}
\begin{equation}
\Phi^{(1)}(x,y,0) = -z_s(x,y)\left.\frac{\partial\Phi^{(0)}}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0}
                  = E_{\rm bg}\,z_s(x,y),
\label{eq:bc_perturbation}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi^{(0)}=-E_{\rm bg}z$ is the planar-conductor solution.
Equation~(\ref{eq:bc_perturbation}) justifies the surface potential
$\phi_s=E_{\rm bg}z_s$ used throughout, valid to $\mathcal{O}(z_s/h)$.
The solution is the half-space Poisson integral
\begin{equation}
\Phi^{(1)}(x,y,z) = \frac{z}{2\pi} \iint
    \frac{E_{\rm bg}\,z_s(x',y')}{[(x-x')^2 + (y-y')^2 + z^2]^{3/2}} \,dx'\,dy',
\label{eq:poisson}
\end{equation}
with Fourier representation
$\tilde{\Phi}^{(1)}(k_x,k_y,z)=E_{\rm bg}\,\tilde{z}_s\,e^{-kz}$,
$k=\sqrt{k_x^2+k_y^2}$.  The perturbation series coincides term by term
with the exact Rayleigh (Floquet) expansion and converges in the domain
$kA\lesssim0.448$~\cite{maystre1971rayleigh,millar1973rayleigh}; the
leading nonlinear correction to $\Delta\omega_x$ scales as
$\mathcal{O}((kA)^2)$ and is verified against the exact solver in
Sec.~\ref{sec:validation} (relative error $0.14\,(kA)^2$).  Higher-order
corrections, the geometric convergence majorant, the ion-induced
self-image term and the equilibrium-position shift are derived in
Supplementary Sec.~S10.

Treating $\Phi^{(1)}$ as a perturbation of the harmonic trapping
potential, the first-order shift of the secular frequency in the
$x$-direction is~\cite{leibfried2003review}
\begin{equation}
\Delta\omega_x = \frac{e}{2m\omega_x}
    \left.\frac{\partial^2\Phi^{(1)}}{\partial x^2}\right|_{(x,y,h)},
\label{eq:dw}
\end{equation}
valid in the perturbative and Lamb--Dicke regimes.  The ion's own charge
induces a self-image correction that modifies the ITF by a factor
$[1-\eta(k,h)]$ with $\eta\le0.2\%$ across the accessible band at
$h=40$\,\textmu m (reaching $9.7\%$ at the $kh=13$ band edge at
$h=5$\,\textmu m, where the full two-term ITF is used in the numerics);
$\eta$ depends only on $(k,h)$, so it does not affect the
topography--charge degeneracy (derivation: Supplementary Sec.~S10).

\subsection{The instrument transfer function}

Combining Eqs.~(\ref{eq:poisson}) and (\ref{eq:dw}) with
$\partial_x^2\to-k_x^2$ in Fourier space gives the forward operator
\begin{equation}
\Delta\tilde{\omega}_x(k_x,k_y) = H_x(k_x,k_y;h)\,
        \tilde{z}_s(k_x,k_y),
\label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
with the \textbf{instrument transfer function}
\begin{equation}
H_x(k_x,k_y;h) = -\frac{e E_{\rm bg}}{2m\omega_x}\,
        k_x^2\,e^{-kh}.
\label{eq:itf}
\end{equation}
The ITF is the product of a high-pass factor $k_x^2$ (the second
derivative of the Poisson kernel) and a low-pass factor $e^{-kh}$ (the
exponential attenuation of the Poisson kernel).  The resulting band-pass
character is a \textbf{distinguishing feature of the ion probe}: for
QESPM the band-pass response is the native, continuous, height-tunable
physical transfer function of the sensor itself, rather than a filter
applied to already measured data (as in the ISO~16610 Gaussian profile
filters~\cite{iso16610}).  Because $H_x$ is real with a constant phase
$\pi$ for $k_x\neq0$, the ITF is a zero-phase filter: the reconstruction
is phase-preserving --- surface features are not spatially shifted by the
measurement, only band-pass filtered (derivation: Supplementary Sec.~S10).

For the 1D cross-section $f(k)\equiv|H_x(k,0;h)|$, the maximum of
$f(k)=Ck^2e^{-kh}$ with $C=eE_{\rm bg}/(2m\omega_x)$ occurs at
\begin{equation}
k_{\rm opt} = \frac{2}{h}, \qquad
    \lambda_{\rm opt} = \pi h,
\label{eq:scaling_kopt}
\end{equation}
with peak value $f_{\rm max}=4Ce^{-2}/h^2$.  The 50\% MTF cutoffs, from
$k^2e^{-kh}=2e^{-2}/h^2$, are
$\lambda_{\min}^{\rm(MTF)}\approx1.51h$ (short-wavelength) with the
long-wavelength cutoff set in practice by the scan range; the passband
quality factor is $Q=2/(x_{\rm hi}-x_{\rm lo})=0.589$ with
$x_{\rm lo}=0.7613$, $x_{\rm hi}=4.1559$ --- a deliberately broad
band-pass filter.  The asymptotic behaviour
$f(k)=Ck^2[1-kh+\mathcal{O}(k^2h^2)]$ for $kh\ll1$ and the
faster-than-any-polynomial decay for $kh\to\infty$ are established in
Supplementary Sec.~S10 together with the sensitivity coefficients
$\partial\ln A/\partial\ln h=-kh$ that underpin the uncertainty budget.

Figure~\ref{fig:itf} shows the ITF magnitude as a function of spatial
frequency for ion heights $h=5$--$100$\,\textmu m.

\subsection{Null space and two-channel operation}

The ITF vanishes for $k_x=0$: the $x$-channel is insensitive to features
varying only in $y$, i.e.\ the null space of $\mathcal{H}_h$ consists of
profiles whose Fourier transform is supported on the line $k_x=0$.  Full
isotropy requires the joint operator $\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_x\oplus
\mathcal{H}_y$; on the subspace of zero-mean functions the joint operator
is injective (its null space consists exactly of the constant mode), and
the combined symbol $|H_{\rm comb}|^2=|H_x|^2+|H_y|^2$ has azimuthal
variation $\sqrt{\cos^4\theta+\sin^4\theta}$ --- a $\sim30\%$ directional
sensitivity variation to be calibrated out in metrological applications
(proof and anisotropy analysis: Supplementary Sec.~S16).  All experimental
QESPM reconstructions should use the joint inversion; the single-channel
analysis of this work is the worst-case (conservative) bound.

\subsection{Forward simulation}

The forward model is implemented on an $N\times N$ grid (pixel
$0.5$\,\textmu m) via the FFT: $\phi_s=E_{\rm bg}z_s$,
$\tilde{\Phi}=\tilde{\phi}_s e^{-kh}$,
$\Delta\tilde{\omega}_x=(e/2m\omega_x)(-k_x^2)\tilde{\Phi}$, inverse FFT.
The chain is exercised on three test surfaces --- topography-only (Gaussian
plateau $200$\,nm, $\sigma=7.5$\,\textmu m; linear ramp $100$\,nm; fine
grating $5$\,nm, $4$\,\textmu m), charge-only (four Gaussian patches of
$\sigma\sim10^{-3}$\,C/m$^2$), and mixed --- with ${}^{40}$Ca$^+$
parameters ($\omega_x/2\pi=1$\,MHz, $E_{\rm bg}=10^4$\,V/m,
$h=40$\,\textmu m; the FFT chain and code are deposited in the
Zenodo repository (see Data Availability).
Figure~\ref{fig:forward} shows the complete forward-model chain.

The surface potential at ion height reaches
$|\Phi|_{\rm max}\approx100$\,\textmu V, and the resulting frequency shift
is $|\Delta\omega_x|/2\pi\approx8.3$\,kHz at peak, consistent with the
analytical ITF prediction applied to the Gaussian plateau (the
$4$\,\textmu m grating is exponentially suppressed as $e^{-kh}$);
$|\Delta\omega_x|/\omega_x<0.9\%$, validating the perturbative treatment.
For the charge-only test case the signal reaches ${\sim}89$\,kHz --- more
than an order of magnitude larger than the topographic signal ---
illustrating the topography--charge degeneracy analysed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:info}.

\subsection{Measurement noise model}

The achievable frequency resolution is limited by three mechanisms
(complete budget and microscopic derivations: Supplementary Secs.~S2 and S15):
(i)~\textbf{quantum projection noise},
$\delta\omega_{\rm QPN}=1/(4\pi\tau\sqrt{N_{\rm rep}\langle n\rangle})$,
which for the optimal probe time $\tau=T_2/2=5$\,ms and
$\langle n\rangle\approx0.5$ gives $\delta\omega_{\rm QPN}/2\pi
\approx0.7$\,Hz --- about $7\times$ below the conservative technical
floor; (ii)~\textbf{motional heating}, $\dot{\bar n}\propto h^{-\alpha}$
with $\alpha\approx3.5$--$4$, giving $\dot{\bar n}\sim10^2$\,quanta/s and
$T_2^{\rm(heat)}\sim10$\,ms at $h=40$\,\textmu m, but $\sim0.8$\,kHz
heating-limited frequency resolution at $h=5$\,\textmu m; and
(iii)~\textbf{rf amplitude fluctuations} contributing
$\delta\omega_{\rm rf}/2\pi\sim1$\,Hz.  Laser frequency noise
contributes ${\sim}11$\,Hz for a $1$\,kHz-linewidth laser (falling to
${\sim}0.1$\,Hz for a $10$\,Hz cavity-stabilised system), and the
Johnson--Nyquist gradient noise is $4\times10^{-8}$\,Hz at $h=40$\,\textmu m,
$T=4$\,K --- negligible across the envelope.  The crossover structure is
the quantitative content of the operating envelope (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_budget}):
at $h\gtrsim10$\,\textmu m the measurement is technical-limited
($5$\,Hz), below $h\sim5$\,\textmu m heating-limited
($\propto h^{-4}$), and the heating--SNR optimum
$h_{\rm opt}=\alpha/k=\alpha\lambda/2\pi$ ($\approx11.8$\,\textmu m for
the Phase-1 grating) is the intersection of the two dominant branches.
Throughout this work SNR values are quoted at the conservative
$\delta\omega/2\pi=5$\,Hz floor --- a stated engineering assumption, not a
measured value.

\section{Spatial information limits and identifiability}
\label{sec:info}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Scaling laws and information loss}

For a sinusoidal surface $z_s(x)=A\sin(kx)$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:itf}) gives the
signal amplitude, the optimal wavenumber, the peak signal, and the minimum
detectable amplitude
\begin{align}
|\Delta\omega_x| &= \frac{eE_{\rm bg}}{2m\omega_x}\,A\,k^2 e^{-kh},
\label{eq:scaling_signal} \\[4pt]
|\Delta\omega_x|_{\rm max} &= \frac{eE_{\rm bg}}{2m\omega_x}\,
        \frac{4A}{h^2}e^{-2},
\label{eq:scaling_peak} \\[4pt]
A_{\rm min}(k,h) &= \frac{2m\omega_x}{eE_{\rm bg}}\,
        \frac{\delta\omega}{k^2 e^{-kh}},
\label{eq:scaling_amin}
\end{align}
where $\delta\omega$ is the frequency measurement precision.  The probe is
most sensitive to surface wavelengths $\lambda\approx\pi h$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:scaling_kopt}); the peak sensitivity degrades as $h^{-2}$
with standoff.  Improving lateral resolution (reducing $h$) improves the
minimum detectable height amplitude quadratically ($\propto h^{-2}$), but
at the cost of exponentially increasing motional heating
(Sec.~\ref{sec:forward}).  For the nominal case ($h=40$\,\textmu m,
$k=2\times10^5$\,rad/m) $A_{\min}\approx1.2$\,nm at the $5$\,Hz floor,
well below the $100$\,nm test amplitude (SNR$\approx80$).

\subsection{Operator theory and effective rank}

In real space $\mathcal{H}_h$ is the convolution operator
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal{H}_h z_s)(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2}
    K_h(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}') \, z_s(\mathbf{r}') \, d^2\mathbf{r}',
\quad
K_h(\mathbf{r}) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}[H_x],
\label{eq:conv_op}
\end{equation}
i.e.~a bounded Fourier multiplier on $L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$ with symbol
$H_x(k_x,k_y;h)$.  Its spectrum is
$\sigma(\mathcal{H}_h)=\overline{\operatorname{ran}H_x}=[-M,0]$ with
$M=(eE_{\rm bg}/2m\omega_x)(4/h^2)e^{-2}$ attained at $k_{\rm opt}=2/h$;
it has no $L^2$ eigenfunctions, its range is non-closed, and the
Moore--Penrose inverse is densely defined but unbounded --- the
functional-analytic statement of severe ill-posedness.  A
translation-invariant operator with non-vanishing symbol is not compact on
the infinite plane; the exponential ill-conditioning follows instead from
the inverse multiplier $H_x^{-1}\propto e^{+kh}/k_x^2$, which amplifies
high-$k$ errors exponentially.  Compactness --- and the discrete singular
spectrum analysed below --- are recovered on the finite periodic lattices
used for all numerical work (extended operator-theoretic analysis:
Supplementary Sec.~S16).

On the finite $N=128$ lattice with spacing $\Delta x=0.5$\,\textmu m
(field of view $L=64$\,\textmu m, the 1D cross-section $k_y=0$), the
discretised forward operator is exactly diagonal in the DFT basis, and
its singular values are the sorted values of $|H_x(k_j;h)|$.  They decay
exponentially, following the Weyl counting law
$\ln(C/\sigma_n h^2)=(\pi h/L)n\,(1+o(1))$
(derivation: Supplementary Sec.~S16), and define the \textbf{effective
rank} $r_{\rm eff}$ --- the number of singular values above
$10^{-3}\sigma_1$ (a conventional but non-unique threshold):

\hline
$h$ [\textmu m] & Effective rank $r_{\rm eff}$ & Shannon information $I$ [nats] \\
\hline

\subsection{Two-channel joint operator}

\begin{proposition}[Injectivity of the joint operator]\label{prop:joint}
On the subspace of zero-mean functions, the joint operator
$\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_x \oplus \mathcal{H}_y$ is injective: its null
space consists exactly of the constant functions.
\end{proposition}

The joint inversion uses the stacked Tikhonov filter; on the
$L=64$\,\textmu m lattice the joint effective rank exceeds the
single-channel rank by a factor $\approx1.3$--$1.5$ at each height, and
the joint reconstruction of the test surface improves the Pearson
correlation from $r=0.939$ (single $x$-channel) to $r=0.969$ (joint).
The joint-operator spectrum and convergence rates are given in
Supplementary Sec.~S16 (Fig.~S23).

\subsection{Theorem: topography--charge identifiability limit}

Real surfaces carry both topographic and charge contributions.  Writing
the surface potential as
$\phi_s(x,y)=E_{\rm bg}z_s(x,y)+\phi_{\rm charge}(x,y)$, a surface charge
distribution $\sigma(x,y)$ on a grounded perfect conductor produces, in
Fourier space,
$\tilde{\phi}_{\rm charge}(k)=\tilde{\sigma}(k)/2\varepsilon_0 k$,
propagating to the ion via the identical $e^{-kh}$ factor
(image-charge derivation: Supplementary Sec.~S10).  The two-parameter
forward model is therefore
\begin{equation}
\Delta\tilde{\omega}_x(k_x,k_y) = H_x(k_x,k_y;h)\,
    \left[\tilde{z}_s(k_x,k_y) + \frac{\tilde{\phi}_{\rm charge}(k_x,k_y)}{E_{\rm bg}}\right],
\label{eq:forward_two_param}
\end{equation}
and a single-height, single-channel measurement cannot separate $z_s$
from $\phi_{\rm charge}/E_{\rm bg}$ without additional assumptions.

\begin{theorem}[Topography--charge identifiability limit]\label{thm:degeneracy}
Let $\Delta\omega_x(k;h)$ be the secular frequency shift
measured at height $h$ for a surface with topographic height $z_s(x)$ and
surface charge distribution $\sigma(x)$.  In the linear regime, both
contributions propagate to the ion via the identical factor $e^{-kh}$.
Consequently, measurements at any finite set of heights $\{h_1, \ldots, h_n\}$
cannot separate $z_s$ from $\sigma$: the $n \times 2$ forward matrix has
rank~1, and its condition number is formally infinite.
\end{theorem}

\textit{Proof sketch.}  The forward model at height $h_j$ is
$\Delta\tilde{\omega}_x(k;h_j)=Ck^2e^{-kh_j}[\tilde{z}_s+\tilde{\sigma}/2\varepsilon_0 k E_{\rm bg}]$,
so each row of the $n\times2$ forward matrix is proportional to
$[1,\,1/2\varepsilon_0 k E_{\rm bg}]$, independent of $h_j$; all rows are
collinear, $\operatorname{rank}=1$ (full proof: Supplementary Sec.~S16).

\begin{corollary}[All electrostatic observables: one scalar field]\label{cor:electrostatic}
Every linear electrostatic observable of the ion--surface system --- the
$x$-, $y$- and $z$-secular channels and the micromotion channel --- is a
linear functional of the single scalar surface field
$\varphi_s(k)=\tilde{z}_s(k)+\tilde{\sigma}(k)/2\varepsilon_0 k E_{\rm bg}$
through a channel-specific weight $W_j(k)e^{-kh_\ell}$: the joint
multi-channel, multi-height forward matrix remains rank~1 in every
Fourier mode, and no combination of electrostatic channels or scan
heights separates topography from charge.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Quantum Cram\'er--Rao bound, degenerate pair]\label{cor:qcrb_degenerate}
At fixed height, the data of any electrostatic channel depend on
$(z_s,\sigma)$ only through $\varphi_s(k)$, so the quantum Fisher
information matrix of the pair is singular (rank~1 per mode) for every
probe state and measurement: quantum enhancement cannot reduce the
identifiability limit of Theorem~\ref{thm:degeneracy}.  Finite uncertainty
for the pair is restored only by prior information or by a
non-electrostatic channel.
\end{corollary}

Three strategies supply the required independent information:
(i)~\textbf{in-situ charge elimination} via ultraviolet
irradiation~\cite{ziomba2025uv} or ion-bombardment cleaning;
(ii)~\textbf{an independent material-contrast channel}: the motional
heating rate $\dot{\bar{n}}$ is a functional of the surface fluctuation
spectrum rather than of the static field, with a spatial kernel of
half-width $0.51h$ --- the same lateral localisation scale as the
electrostatic channel --- and is not annihilated by the linear rank-1
degeneracy (kernel derivation: Supplementary Sec.~S15); whether it
restores practical joint identifiability is an open question requiring
the nonlinear joint analysis; and
(iii)~\textbf{Bayesian joint inversion} with distinct spatial priors
(smoothness for topography, sparsity for charge), analysed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:precision}.  This degeneracy is the central fundamental
challenge of the technique and is shared with all scanning electrostatic
probes (Kelvin probe, EFM).

\subsection{Stability and the broadband roughness response}

The surface-induced force gradient must remain below the trapping
potential: the stability criterion is $|\Delta\omega_x|<\omega_x/2$, with
critical amplitude growing rapidly with height
($A_{\rm crit}(h)\propto e^{+2\pi h/\lambda}$ at fixed $\lambda$); the
linear ITF applies over the nominal regime
$h\approx5$--$200$\,\textmu m, and at the nominal point
($h=40$\,\textmu m, $A=100$\,nm) the stability margin exceeds two orders
of magnitude (stability diagram: Supplementary Sec.~S12).  For broadband
roughness characterised by the ISO~25178 parameter $R_a$, the RMS secular
shift is \textbf{linear in $R_a$} for $R_a\ll h$ --- a Parseval identity
makes the linear scaling exact for stationary Gaussian surfaces --- with a
roughness sensitivity of $68$\,Hz/nm at $h=40$\,\textmu m rising to
$4.3\times10^3$\,Hz/nm at $h=5$\,\textmu m: QESPM acts as a calibratable
non-contact roughness gauge (full analysis: Supplementary Sec.~S13).

\subsection{Quantum Fisher information and the precision limit}

The ion's $x$-motion is the oscillator $H_0=\hbar\omega_x a^\dagger a$;
the surface amplitude $A$ is encoded in $\Delta\omega_x$ through the ITF,
$\partial\Delta\omega_x/\partial A=H_x(k;h)/A$ for a sinusoid.  A
frequency measurement of duration $\tau$ has fundamental precision set by
the quantum Cram\'er--Rao bound~\cite{giovannetti2011advances,degen2017quantum},
saturated by Ramsey interferometry on the motional state.  For coherent,
squeezed-vacuum ($r=1.5$, $\sim13$\,dB) and NOON ($N=100$) probe states
the quantum Fisher information per spatial mode factorises as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_A(k;h) = |H_x(k;h)|^2 \, \mathcal{F}_{\rm ion},
\label{eq:FQ_factorisation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_{\rm ion}=4|\alpha|^2\tau^2$ (coherent, the classical
limit), $e^{2r}\mathcal{F}_{\rm ion}^{\rm coh}$ (squeezed) and
$N^2\mathcal{F}^{(1)}$ (NOON, Heisenberg limit).  At equal average quanta
$\langle n\rangle=|\alpha|^2=N=100$ the enhancement factors are
$\times e^{r}\approx4.5$ (squeezing) and $\times\sqrt{N}=10$ (NOON),
independent of the absolute noise floor.  These enhancements are
\textbf{multiplicative} with the classical sensitivity set by the ITF and
the noise floor: they amplify precision within the accessible band but do
not extend the band itself.  This is a central result of the paper:
\textbf{quantum enhancement can maximise the precision with which an
available signal is measured, but it cannot recover spatial information
that has already been exponentially attenuated by the physical transfer
function.}  At the QPN limit
($\delta\omega_{\rm QPN}/2\pi\approx0.7$\,Hz), the coherent-state
sensitivity reaches $\delta A_{\min}^{\rm coh}\sim7\times10^{-3}$\,nm at
$k_{\rm opt}=2/h$, improving by ${\sim}4.5$ (squeezed) and ${\sim}10$
(NOON); under the conservative $5$\,Hz floor the values are ${\sim}7\times$
larger.  Under dephasing, NOON states lose their advantage when
$N\gamma$ approaches $1/\tau_{\rm opt}$; the coherent-state protocol with
$\tau\sim T_2/2$ is the robust near-term target (multiparameter QCRB,
optimal measurement and resource allocation: Supplementary Sec.~S11).
Figure~\ref{fig:qfi} shows the quantum-limited amplitude sensitivity and
the enhancement factors.

\subsection{The distinction between sensitivity and reconstruction uncertainty}

Three fundamentally different quantities must be distinguished throughout:
(i)~\textbf{detection sensitivity} $\delta A_{\min}$ --- the smallest
surface amplitude detectable above the noise floor, directly improved by
quantum enhancement;
(ii)~\textbf{height reconstruction uncertainty} $u_c(z)$ --- the GUM-based
combined standard uncertainty of a reconstructed height value, dominated
by systematic effects (surface charge) that quantum enhancement cannot
reduce; and
(iii)~\textbf{lateral spatial resolution} --- quantified by the 50\% MTF
short-wavelength cutoff $\lambda_{\min}^{\rm(MTF)}\approx1.51h$, governed
by the $e^{-kh}$ decay of the ITF.  Confusing these overstates the
instrument: the picometre-scale detection figures are sensitivity figures,
while the $319$\,nm budget below is an identifiability figure.

Table~\ref{tab:regimes} compares the four measurement regimes at the
nominal ion height, showing that quantum enhancement improves
$\delta A_{\min}$ linearly with $\delta\omega$, while the effective rank
and the geometric MTF cutoff are \textbf{identical} across all charge-free
regimes and the noise-limited cutoff improves only logarithmically:
quantum enhancement buys sensitivity, not spatial information.  At the
same time, \textbf{charge elimination provides a ${\sim}2.7\times10^2\times$
improvement} --- far larger than any quantum enhancement --- establishing
the central practical conclusion that the dominant bottleneck is
electrostatic identifiability, not measurement precision.

\subsection{Regularised inversion}

For the topography-only case the inverse problem reduces to Fourier
deconvolution.  The Tikhonov-regularised estimate of the surface potential
at ion height is
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda(k_x,k_y) = \frac{H_x^*}{|H_x|^2 + \lambda^2}\,
    \Delta\tilde{\omega}_x(k_x,k_y),
\label{eq:tikhonov}
\end{equation}
with the height recovered via
$\tilde{z}_s=\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda e^{+kh}/E_{\rm bg}$.  The discrete
Picard condition (the Fourier coefficients of the noise-free data must
decay faster than the singular values) connects the effective rank of
Sec.~\ref{sec:info} directly to the regularisation theory: for noisy data
the condition is violated beyond $r_{\rm eff}$, where noise dominates.
The empirical regularisation parameter $\lambda=10^{-4}\max|H_x|$ lies
between the L-curve and generalised cross-validation choices; the
discrepancy principle, L-curve and GCV analyses are given in
Supplementary Sec.~S16.  On a source set $X_s(E)$ of zero-mean surfaces
with $\|z\|_{H^s}\le E$, the inverse problem is conditionally
(logarithmically) stable: for data of noise level $\delta$,
\begin{equation}
\|z_1-z_2\|_{L^2} \le \bigl(1+C_1E\bigr)\delta^{1/3}
    + C_2 E\,h^{s}\,\bigl(\ln(1/\delta)\bigr)^{-s}\,\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),
\label{eq:cond_stability_rate}
\end{equation}
and the minimax lower bound matches this logarithmic rate --- no method
can do better than the $(\ln(1/\delta))^{-s}$ factor imposed by the
$e^{-kh}$ transfer function~\cite{engl1996inverse,hohage2000regularization}
(proofs: Supplementary Sec.~S16).  The practical implication: halving the
measurement noise improves the reconstruction error only by a fixed
additive constant in the exponent; practical gains come primarily from
reducing $h$.

The Tikhonov filter is the posterior mean of a Bayesian model with a
Gaussian-process prior over surfaces; the prior supplies the
frequency-dependent regulariser with no free parameter,
\begin{equation}
\hat z_k = \frac{H_x(k)^*\,d_k}{|H_x(k)|^2 + \sigma_{\rm pix}^2
\Delta x^2/\Sigma(k)},
\label{eq:bayesian_post}
\end{equation}
achieving $r=0.933$ on the test surface (statistically equivalent to the
hand-tuned scalar Tikhonov, $r=0.939$) while providing the full posterior
covariance.  For the topography--charge pair, the per-mode posterior
precision is rank-1 in the data term, and the marginal topography
variance saturates at the charge-prior floor
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}(z_k|d)\to \frac{p_\varphi}{E_{\rm bg}^2}
\qquad\text{(strong data)},
\label{eq:joint_post_var}
\end{equation}
regardless of measurement quality: under the GUM charge prior
$u(\sigma)=1.13\times10^{-8}$\,C/m$^2$, the charge-prior-limited
baseline uncertainty is
$\sqrt{p_\varphi}/E_{\rm bg}=319$\,nm --- exactly the dominant charge row
of Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty}.  The Bayesian posterior and the GUM
budget are two views of the same degeneracy; distinct priors separate the
channels only to the extent that the charge prior itself is tightened.
The GP-prior reconstruction, the joint posterior analysis and the
synthetic joint topography--charge demonstration (joint inversion
recovers the charge field with $r=0.926$ and improves the topography to
$r=0.791$) are given in Supplementary Sec.~S16.

\section{Numerical validation and realistic surface reconstruction}
\label{sec:validation}
% ===================================================================

The results of Secs.~\ref{sec:forward}--\ref{sec:precision} were obtained
by applying the forward model and its inverse to synthetic surfaces
generated within the same theoretical framework.  This section breaks the
circularity: the forward model is cross-checked against an exact
electrostatic solver hierarchy and evaluated on experimentally measured
surface topography.

\subsection{Independent solver hierarchy}

The first-order boundary perturbation expansion is validated against the
exact Rayleigh (Floquet) solution of the Laplace equation with a
corrugated Dirichlet boundary,
$\Phi(x,z)=-E_{\rm bg}z+\sum_n c_n\sin(nkx)e^{-nkz}$, with the
coefficients fixed by the exact boundary condition
$\Phi(x,A\sin(kx))=0$.  For a sinusoidal surface of period
$\lambda=40$\,\textmu m, the ITF reproduces the exact secular frequency
shift with relative error $0.01\%$ at $A=100$\,nm, growing as
$0.14\,(kA)^2$ to $1.4\%$ at $A=2$\,\textmu m.  An independent
boundary-element (single-layer collocation) solver reproduces the exact
Rayleigh result to $\le0.6\%$ across the amplitude range without using
the ITF, and an independent finite-difference solver on the corrugated
domain converges toward the ITF prediction with vertical grid refinement
--- three mutually independent solvers confirming the first-order
result.  Convergence studies, solver settings and the solver-hierarchy
figure are in Supplementary Sec.~S3.

\subsection{Synthetic-surface reconstruction}

On synthetic surfaces combining a calibrated sinusoidal grating, a
Gaussian plateau and broadband power-law roughness --- none of which are
simple sinusoids for which the inversion is analytically exact --- the
Tikhonov-regularised reconstruction at $h=40$\,\textmu m achieves
$r=0.981$ for the resolvable features (grating and plateau,
$\lambda>\lambda_{\min}$), with the residual dominated by the fine
grating ($\lambda=4$\,\textmu m) below the 50\% MTF cutoff
$\lambda_{\min}\approx60$\,\textmu m (Fig.~\ref{fig:inversion}).  This
self-consistency check confirms that the FFT forward/inverse chain is
numerically correct; it does not constitute an independent validation.
The reconstruction quality follows $r=0.838$ at $h=5$\,\textmu m
improving to $r=0.992$ at $h=80$\,\textmu m.

steel~\cite{camargo2019afm} --- a conducting surface compatible with the
perfect-conductor assumption --- is propagated through the ITF with the
$5$\,Hz floor added to the simulated ion-signal maps.  \textbf{This is a
numerical evaluation using experimentally measured topography, not an
experimental validation of QESPM:} the ion signal is simulated with the
same forward model that is subsequently inverted, so the correlation
improvement with decreasing $h$ is a mathematically necessary consequence
of the $e^{-kh}$ filter; the independent validation is provided by the
exact solvers of Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}.  The measurement is a Bruker
NanoScope scan ($512\times512$ pixels, $20\times20$\,\textmu m) with

result (Fig.~\ref{fig:real_afm}): the Pearson correlation between the AFM

independently measured surface topography as input; at

constraints --- $r=0.76$ is achieved and the dominant features are
visually recovered.  The $h=2$\,\textmu m point is a theoretical
extrapolation beyond current feasibility (heating
$\dot{\bar n}\sim5\times10^6$\,quanta/s), included to illustrate the
asymptotic ITF behaviour.  The full input data, ion-signal maps and

\subsection{Comparison with published ion-probe data}

For the Maiwald et al.~\cite{maiwald2009stylus} stylus-trap configuration

$|\Delta\omega_x|/2\pi\sim3.5$\,MHz against the observed ${\sim}10$\,kHz
--- a factor ${\sim}350$ consistent with strong rf-electrode shielding in
that geometry; this is an order-of-magnitude consistency check, not a
quantitative validation.  For the S{\"a}gesser et
al.~\cite{sagesser2026scanning} 3D scanning platform
(${}^{40}$Ca$^+$, $E_{\rm bg}\sim10^4$\,V/m), the ITF predicts
$|\Delta\omega_x|/2\pi\sim65$\,kHz at $h=10$\,\textmu m and a signal below
the $5$\,Hz floor at $h=100$\,\textmu m
(Fig.~\ref{fig:published}).

\subsection{Analytical benchmarks, robustness and Monte Carlo checks}

An implementation-free analytical benchmark computes the step-edge
response in closed form from the Poisson integral: for a
$\Delta z=200$\,nm step, the edge spread function
$\Delta\omega_x(x)=-(eE_{\rm bg}\Delta z/2m\omega_x)(2/\pi)\,xh/(x^2+h^2)^2$
has extrema at $x=\pm h/\sqrt{3}$ and FWHM ${\approx}0.34h$; the FFT
implementation agrees to relative RMS error $2.3\times10^{-3}$ (maximum
$3.9\times10^{-3}$) over the masked edge region.  Three distinct lateral
metrics follow from this single edge response and are used consistently
throughout: the PSF first zero $x_0=h/\sqrt{3}$ (central-lobe scale), the
Rayleigh-style two-point criterion $\Delta x_{\rm res}=2h/\sqrt3
\approx1.15h$, and the 50\% MTF cutoff
$\lambda_{\min}^{\rm(MTF)}\approx1.51h$ --- an ESF shape parameter, a
two-point separation criterion and a spatial-frequency cutoff, each
scaling linearly with $h$ (derivations and the 3D Gaussian-bump
benchmark: Supplementary Sec.~S6).

Robustness to model violation is quantified in Supplementary Sec.~S4: a
$d=2$\,\textmu m dielectric overlayer ($\varepsilon_r=2$--$10$) enhances
apparent heights by $+9\%$ to $+40\%$ across the passband; an $E_{\rm bg}$
gradient of $10\%$--$50\%$ across the field of view produces correlation
loss $1-r$ up to $0.04$; large surface slope ($Ak\gtrsim0.1$) breaks the
linear inversion, with nonlinear (Landweber) inversion restoring accuracy.
A full Monte Carlo propagation (JCGM 101:2008, Supplement 1,
$5\times10^3$ realisations) reproduces the GUM budget --- combined
standard uncertainty $\approx300$\,nm at the grating crest (GUM
single-point value $319$\,nm) --- and a coverage check over
$2\times10^4$ realisations places $95.1\%$ of true heights inside the
propagated $95\%$ interval ($\pm1.96\,u_c$, $u_c=319$\,nm) with an
estimate bias of $0.7$\,nm: the nominal 95\% interval achieves 95.1\%
empirical coverage in this simulation under the stated input
distributions (details: Supplementary Sec.~S5).

\section{Operating envelope, limitations and experimental implementation}
\label{sec:envelope}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Operating envelope}

The QESPM operating envelope is defined in the three-dimensional space
$(h,\lambda,A)$ of ion height, surface wavelength and amplitude, bounded
by (i) the 50\% MTF short-wavelength cutoff
$\lambda_{\min}^{\rm(MTF)}\approx1.51h$; (ii) the FOV-limited
long-wavelength cutoff $\lambda_{\max}=L_{\rm scan}$; (iii) the minimum
detectable amplitude $A_{\min}(k,h)$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:scaling_amin}); and
(iv) the stability boundary $A_{\rm crit}(h)$ (Supplementary Sec.~S12).
The regime combines non-contact measurement, micrometre-scale standoff
($h>1$\,\textmu m) and high intrinsic displacement sensitivity
($\delta z<1$\,nm).  Figure~\ref{fig:landscape} positions QESPM in the
detection sensitivity--standoff plane: it provides sub-nanometre vertical
detection sensitivity (picometre with motional squeezing at the QPN
limit) over the modelled micrometre-scale standoff regime
($h=1$--$100$\,\textmu m as the explored numerical range) --- orders of
magnitude larger than AFM or STM --- while operating natively in the UHV
environment of the ion trap; the realistic topographic reconstruction
uncertainty, by contrast, is charge-limited at ${\sim}319$\,nm
(Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty}).  To our knowledge, no experimentally
demonstrated surface-metrology method currently combines micrometre-standoff
non-contact operation, cryogenic UHV compatibility and projected
quantum-limited detection sensitivity; none is free of the electrostatic
identifiability limit either.

\subsection{Limitations}

The principal limitations of QESPM are: (i) lateral resolution set by the
50\% MTF short-wavelength cutoff
$\lambda_{\min}^{\rm(MTF)}\approx1.51h$, with the Rayleigh-style
two-point criterion $\approx1.15h$ as a separate two-point metric,
restricting nanometre-scale lateral feature detection unless the ion is
brought to sub-micrometre heights (where Casimir--Polder forces and
heating become problematic); (ii) the UHV requirement restricts sample
types; (iii) scan throughput is low --- an order-of-magnitude estimate of
${\sim}2.5\times10^{-1}$\,\textmu m$^2$/s for a $64\times64$\,\textmu m$^2$
field on a $128\times128$ grid at $1$\,s/pixel, versus
${\sim}10^{4}$\,\textmu m$^2$/s for commercial optical profilometry, an
illustrative comparison rather than a universal benchmark; and (iv) a
conducting surface (or thin dielectric on a conductor) is required.
Future directions include quantum-enhanced sensitivity via squeezed or
Fock-state interferometry, exploitation of the Casimir--Polder force at
$h<100$\,nm as an independent short-range channel, integration as an
in-situ diagnostic in surface-electrode trap quantum
processors~\cite{kumph2016operation}, and physics-informed inversion for
real-time topography--charge separation.

\subsection{Experimental roadmap}

A four-phase roadmap is specified in Supplementary Sec.~S9:
(1) static demonstration at $h\le30$\,\textmu m above a certified grating
(ITF predicts $122$\,Hz at $h=30$\,\textmu m, $2.8$\,kHz at
$h=20$\,\textmu m, $65$\,kHz at $h=10$\,\textmu m; confirming the
$k^2e^{-kh}$ height scaling to $\sim10\%$ would constitute the first
experimental test of the framework, requiring no component not already
demonstrated in the S{\"a}gesser platform); (2) 1D scanning; (3) 2D
scanning with full inversion; and (4) inter-laboratory comparison and
submission to ISO/TC~213.  Per-phase signal levels, coherence times,
dominant noise branches, risks and mitigations are specified in
Supplementary Sec.~S9.  Sequential two-pass measurement strategies
(coarse scan at large $h$, fine scan at small $h$, with D-optimal point
selection protected by a $(1-e^{-1})\approx0.63$ submodularity guarantee)
are information-efficient under the stated model and acquisition
assumptions: a 1D demonstration reduces the amplitude error from $0.071$
(uniform 30-point) to $0.028$ (adaptive) at a quarter of the
exhaustive-scan time (analysis: Supplementary Sec.~S14).

The closest physical predecessors are single-ion electric-field sensors:
rf-photon-correlation electrometry reaches field sensitivities near
$10$\,mV/m/$\sqrt{\rm Hz}$~\cite{keller2015electrometry}, and 2D
ion-crystal platforms demonstrate quantum-enhanced field sensing at the
$10$\,dB level~\cite{gilmore2021quantum}.  QESPM differs categorically:
(i) it senses the field gradient (second derivative of the potential)
through the secular frequency, so its observable carries
spatial-frequency selectivity (the ITF) absent from a point field probe;
(ii) it reconstructs a map through regularised inversion of a severely
ill-posed operator; and (iii) its metrological deliverable is
SI-traceable surface texture, not field strength.  The
sensitivity comparison on each method's native observable is given in
Supplementary Sec.~S5 (Table~S2).  Scanning ion conductance microscopy
(SICM)~\cite{leitao2021sicm} is complementary: liquid-phase operation,
resolution set by the pipette aperture rather than $e^{-kh}$ filtering.

\subsection{The topography--charge identifiability limit in context}

The identifiability limit of Theorem~\ref{thm:degeneracy} is not unique
to the ion probe: Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM)~\cite{glatzel2022kelvin}
and electrostatic force microscopy (EFM)~\cite{serry2000efm} suffer the
same cross-talk, conventionally resolved by two-pass scanning (mechanical
topography in the first pass, potential measurement at a lift height in
the second); the purely non-contact ion probe precludes mechanical
contact, demanding a different strategy.  The relationship is stated
precisely.  \emph{Similarity:} all three techniques sense electrostatic
surface information and face topography--potential cross-talk.
\emph{Fundamental difference:} QESPM is a stationary quantum probe with
no cantilever and no mechanical interaction, operating at micrometre
standoff through a frequency measurement of a quantum oscillator,
natively compatible with UHV and cryogenic environments, with a spatial
response governed by the analytic transfer function $k_x^2e^{-kh}$ ---
none of which applies to KPFM/EFM.  \emph{Shared limitation:} all three
inherit an identifiability (cross-talk) problem between geometric and
electrostatic contrast, so the mitigation strategies of
Sec.~\ref{sec:info} apply beyond QESPM.

\subsection{Connection to anomalous heating in ion traps}

The microscopic origin of the electric-field noise driving anomalous ion
heating near surfaces has been debated for two decades (reviewed by
Brownnutt et al.~\cite{brownnutt2015review}); the noise-source
distribution has not, to date, been spatially resolved experimentally,
because heating-rate measurements integrate over the entire electrode
area.  QESPM offers a direct solution: the heating rate
$\dot{\bar n}(x,y)$ can be mapped simultaneously with
$\Delta\omega_x(x,y)$, and its spatial autocorrelation yields the
patch-potential correlation length $\ell_c$ --- a parameter appearing in
microscopic trap-heating studies~\cite{daniilidis2011fabrication} and
adsorbate-diffusion models~\cite{kim2017noise}.  A measurement of
$\ell_c\sim10$--$100$\,nm would support the patch-potential model;
$\ell_c\sim1$--$10$\,\textmu m would point toward adsorbate diffusion.
This positions QESPM not merely as a surface metrology technique, but as
a diagnostic tool for one of the central unresolved problems in
trapped-ion quantum information science.

\subsection{Potential classification within the ISO~25178 framework}

ISO~25178-6 classifies areal surface texture measurement methods by
physical principle (stylus, optical, scanning probe).  The proposed
QESPM measurement principle has characteristics that could motivate
consideration as a distinct physical-principle subclass: a distinct
physical principle (long-range electrostatic potential sensing via
quantum state readout), a defined and calibratable ITF
(Eq.~\ref{eq:itf}), a traceable five-step calibration procedure, and a
quantified GUM-based uncertainty budget.  Formal classification would
require experimental validation, calibration studies and
inter-laboratory comparison --- none of which has yet been performed ---
projected to become available within 3--4 years of the first
experimental demonstration.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ===================================================================

This paper has addressed a fundamental question in quantum surface
metrology: how much spatial information about a conducting surface can be
extracted by a single quantum probe?  The answer, established through the
complete theoretical framework developed above, is that the instrument
transfer function $H_x(k;h)\propto k_x^2 e^{-kh}$ imposes an irreducible
spatial-information cutoff at $\lambda\sim h$ --- and that no amount of
quantum enhancement can recover spatial frequencies already attenuated by
the $e^{-kh}$ physical filter.

Three key results support this conclusion:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The factorisation $\mathcal{F}_A(k;h)=|H_x|^2\,
          \mathcal{F}_{\rm ion}$} (Eq.~\ref{eq:FQ_factorisation}) cleanly
          separates quantum-metrological enhancement from the electrostatic
          transfer limit: as $kh\to\infty$, $\mathcal{F}_A\to0$ regardless
          of $\mathcal{F}_{\rm ion}$.
    \item \textbf{The regime comparison} (Table~\ref{tab:regimes})
          demonstrates that the effective rank and the geometric MTF
          cutoff are identical across classical, QPN, squeezed and
          Heisenberg-limited measurement, while the noise-limited cutoff
          improves only logarithmically --- quantum enhancement improves
          sensitivity but not spatial information.
    \item \textbf{The topography--charge identifiability limit}
          (Theorem~\ref{thm:degeneracy}) shows that multi-height scanning
          alone can never separate geometric from electrostatic surface
          features --- a fundamental limitation shared by all non-contact
          electrostatic probes.
\end{enumerate}

The practical path forward is clear: motional squeezing ($\times4.5$)
provides the most robust near-term quantum enhancement, while in-situ
charge elimination addresses the dominant systematic uncertainty.  Charge
mitigation could substantially reduce the present charge-limited
reconstruction uncertainty, but the resulting performance remains to be
established experimentally.

Beyond its immediate application as a surface metrology tool, QESPM
establishes a broader principle: \textbf{quantum sensors are ultimately
limited not by measurement noise but by the physical transfer function
that couples the object of interest to the quantum probe.}  This principle
applies equally to NV-centre magnetometry, trapped-ion electrometry, and
emerging quantum imaging platforms, and merits systematic investigation
across the quantum sensing landscape.
